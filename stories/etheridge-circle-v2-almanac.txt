========================================================================
THE ETHERIDGE CIRCLE, 1891
========================================================================

In the autumn of 1891 the influenza was expected back, Mr. Parnell was newly dead, the
Theosophists were still in mourning for Madame Blavatsky, and in Cheltenham the new
theatre had opened with Mrs. Langtry. On the first Tuesday after Michaelmas, Mrs.
Lavinia Ashworth of Lansdown, who collects mediums as other women collect china, wrote
to her neighbour Mrs. Helena Marchmont, a widow eighteen months bereaved of her only
son, to say that she had engaged a young sensitive from Bath, a Miss Clara Vane, for
her Tuesday sitting, and that she would take it very kindly if Helena came.

Helena had refused such invitations before. This time she went.

Present in Mrs. Ashworth's drawing-room on the evening of Tuesday the thirteenth of
October were Mrs. Ashworth, Mrs. Marchmont, her companion, two neighbours of good
family, and Miss Vane. Dr. Arthur Pembroke of London, an old friend of the late Mr.
Marchmont, had been asked and had declined.

After some minutes of the usual phenomena, Miss Vane, in trance, spoke in a voice the
company took to be that of Mrs. Marchmont's son. Among other things the voice said:

    "The blue ribbon was not burnt in Heaven."

Mrs. Marchmont was seen to go white and asked for the sitting to end. She has since
told nobody what the words meant to her, and she has not been able to decide whether
what she felt in that room was faith or its opposite. Within the week the matter had
reached Dr. Pembroke, and, by way of Mrs. Ashworth's dinner-table, Mr. Julian Strake
of the London weeklies.

What follows is their correspondence.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 1: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
14 October 1891

My dear Dr. Pembroke,

You will say, I daresay, that you warned me, though in fact you did no more than decline Mrs. Ashworth's invitation with your usual courteous firmness, and left me to make my own error. I went, on Tuesday last, to her sitting in Lansdown. I had resolved a dozen times not to, and then the day came round and I found myself putting on my bonnet as though someone else's hand were on the strings.

 I will not pretend to you, of all people, that nothing occurred. Something was said in that darkened room — I cannot set it down even to you without a kind of cold creeping over me — that touched upon a private matter of mine so exactly, so cruelly exactly, that I have not been able to think of anything else since. Mrs. Ashworth, of course, has already told half of Lansdown that 'dear Mrs. Marchmont received a most striking communication,' which is precisely the sort of indiscretion I dreaded, and precisely why I did not write to you sooner.

 I do not ask you to believe. I know your opinion of these things, and I own I have resented it more than once, thinking it merely the comfortable scepticism of a man who has not stood where I stand. But I ask you now, as an old friend of my husband's and mine, to tell me plainly, and without contempt for my folly, what you suppose can explain such a thing — trickery, chance, or something in the mind itself that we do not yet understand. I find I can no longer tell whether what I felt in that room was faith, or the very collapse of it.

 The town, meanwhile, talks of nothing but Mrs. Langtry and the new theatre, and the doctors forbid us all the evening air. I might have done better to heed them.

I remain, dependent upon your candour as ever,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 2: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
16 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

You do me an injustice, though a gentle one, in supposing I should crow over you. I have had no inclination to crow this week at all; the papers are bordered in black for Mr. Parnell and Mr. Smith together, the fog has come down early and thick upon the river, and one is reminded, whatever one's opinions, how little margin there is between the living and the rest of us. It is not a season for triumph over a friend's distress.

I will own that your phrase — 'a private matter of mine so exactly' — has stayed with me rather more than I like to confess, and I find I cannot offer you the plain clinical answer you ask for until I know a good deal more precisely what was said. Not the effect it had upon you, which I can too easily imagine, but the words themselves, as nearly as memory allows — what was named, what was implied, whether Mrs. Ashworth or the medium spoke first, and what, if anything, was asked of you beforehand. You need not spare my scepticism in the telling; I promise to spare you my contempt, which I never truly felt for you, only for the company you had put yourself in.

Mrs. Ashworth, I am sorry to hear, has done exactly what her nature obliges her to do, and told Lansdown what she ought to have kept for your ear alone. That indiscretion alone should teach you how little the room can be trusted, whatever moved in it.

You ask whether it was trickery, chance, or something in the mind we do not yet understand. It may be any of the three, but I cannot judge which until you trust me with the particulars, as you have always done before. Write to me plainly, my dear Mrs. Marchmont — I would rather bear the whole weight of it than have you carry it alone into another such evening.

I remain, as ever, your faithful and concerned friend,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 3: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
18 October 1891

My dear Dr. Pembroke,

You ask for the words themselves, and I find I owe you that much, though I confess the asking costs me something. The room was darkened, as they always are — Mrs. Ashworth insists it aids what she calls the 'conditions' — and the young person who sat for us, a Miss Clara Vane, lately come to Cheltenham and much spoken of among Mrs. Ashworth's circle, said very little at first beyond the ordinary murmurings one expects. Then, after some minutes, she said, in a voice not quite her own, that there was 'one who had gone before his time, and was not easy, though not unhappy,' and that he wished his mother to know he had 'kept nothing back that mattered.' There was more of that general kind, the sort of thing, I own, that might be said to any bereaved woman in any darkened room in England.

But there was one particular — a small thing, a mere object named, a ribbon, blue, such as might tie up any bundle of letters or keepsakes in any house — that struck me only because it happened to answer to something in my own housekeeping, nothing more. I tell you this so that you may see how little there truly was to build upon, and how much a mind already disposed to grief will supply of its own accord. Nothing was asked of me beforehand beyond the usual sitting fee for the poor fund Mrs. Ashworth patronises, and no name was given until Miss Vane herself, unprompted, said 'Arthur.'

I have set it down as fairly as memory allows, though I will not pretend memory is a cold instrument in such an hour. You will tell me, I expect, that a name so common and an object so ordinary prove nothing but my own readiness to be moved. I should like, for once, to hear you say I am wrong.

I remain, with more trust in your discretion than I sometimes own,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 4: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
20 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

You have kept your word and given me the particulars, and I thank you for it, though I own the reading of them cost me a night's sleep I could ill spare. I will give you the plain answer you ask for, so far as I am able.

A name so common as Arthur signifies nothing by itself — there are ten thousand Arthurs in England, living and dead, and a medium who deals in such names deals safely, for the sitter will always find one to fit. As for the ribbon, blue and tied about letters, you will recall that such things are the very stock-in-trade of the sitting-room prophet: vague enough to be denied, particular enough to be seized upon by a mind already softened by grief. Mr. Myers and Mr. Podmore have lately been at each other's throats in the Society's Proceedings over exactly this — whether such 'hits' prove anything beyond the sitter's own readiness to supply the meaning. I incline, as you know, to Podmore's colder view.

But a cold view is not a complete one, and I find I cannot leave the matter there. You tell me Miss Vane is 'lately come to Cheltenham.' I should like very much to know from where, and by whose introduction she first appeared in Mrs. Ashworth's drawing-room, and whether she has had any acquaintance, however slight, with your household or its former members — a servant once in your employ, a nurse, a tradesman's family, anyone who might have carried tales without meaning harm by it. You will think this a physician's excess of caution, and perhaps it is; but I have seen more mischief come of an idle housemaid's gossip than of any spirit, and I would rather run down that hare than leave you to imagine a ghost where there is only a chattering town.

Do not go again to Lansdown until you have written to me. The fog is thick enough on the river without your adding to it in that darkened parlour.

I remain, with more concern than I can well express, your faithful friend,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 5: Miss Clara Vane to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bath
22 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

I have thought of you constantly since Tuesday, and of the sitting at Mrs. Ashworth's, which has left me strangely tired, as such evenings always do when something true has passed through me. You know I never speak lightly of what comes in that state; I would rather be silent a twelvemonth than utter a word merely to please. And yet I could not be silent then. The name, the little blue ribbon — I did not choose them, dear Mrs. Marchmont, any more than one chooses the weather. They came, and I gave them, and I have paid for it since in a headache that has not left me three days together.

The rain here has been dreadful, the river up over the walk by the weir again, and half my acquaintance laid low already with the autumn chill, so that I begin to dread the winter influenza before it has properly begun. It is a poor time to be alone with one's thoughts, and I confess I have felt very alone, wondering whether what I said gave you comfort or only opened an old wound I had no right to touch. If it was the latter, you must tell me plainly; I would rather bear your anger than your silence.

Mrs. Ashworth, as you know, cannot keep a thing to herself, and I gather already that Dr. Pembroke has heard some garbled account of the evening. I do not fear his opinion of me — I have had colder looks than his — but I should be sorry if it coloured his opinion of you, or made him think you credulous, which you are not. You have always seen further than most into these matters, and it is that discernment in you I value above any fee. Write to me when you are able, and tell me how you do, and whether the ribbon meant what I think it did.

I remain, with true affection and respect, yours,
Clara Vane


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 6: Mr. Julian Strake to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
24 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

I hope you will forgive a note from one who has, until now, known you chiefly through the kindness of others' report — Mrs. Ashworth, in particular, who last week gave me, over her excellent but rather too strong claret, an account of an evening at your house that I have not been able to put from my mind since.

I do not write as one of the curious herd who besiege every drawing-room where grief and the unseen are said to have met. I write, if I am honest, as a man who has grown weary of the fog — and we have had the first real fogs of the season down by the river this week, thick enough to stop the traffic on the Embankment and fill the hospitals with the usual autumn coughing — and who finds in your circle something rather more substantial than the ordinary traffic in ghosts. The Society for Psychical Research has just issued a new part of its Proceedings, and Mr. Myers and Mr. Podmore are, as ever, tearing each other's evidence to pieces in print; it strikes me that the whole question wants a calmer, more human voice than either of theirs.

I should be glad, if you permit it, to call upon you — not to catechise you, still less to write you up for some Sunday paper, but simply to hear, in your own words, what passed that Tuesday, and what you yourself believe you heard. Mrs. Ashworth's account, delivered secondhand and at some volume on account of her poor ear, cannot have done it justice.

I remain conscious that grief is not a subject for a stranger's curiosity, and if you would rather I did not come, you need only say so, and I shall not press it.

Believe me, with sincere respect, yours faithfully,
Julian Strake


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 7: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
26 October 1891

My dear Dr. Pembroke,

I write before I have answered a letter that has unsettled me more than I like to admit, because I find I would rather have your counsel first than trust my own judgment, which you will say — not unkindly, I hope — has not always served me well of late.

A Mr. Julian Strake has written to me, a literary gentleman, I gather, who has had the whole matter of Tuesday's sitting from Mrs. Ashworth over her claret, as she gives everything, with more volume than accuracy. He speaks of the Society's Proceedings, of Mr. Myers and Mr. Podmore, and asks leave to call upon me and hear 'in my own words' what passed — not, he assures me, to catechise or to write me up for a Sunday paper, though I confess a man who mentions the possibility at all has already half admitted the temptation. I do not know what to make of him. His prose is agreeable, which frightens me rather more than it flatters me, for I have found agreeable men easier to trust than they deserve.

Miss Vane has also written, very tenderly, fearing she has opened an old wound, and asking whether the ribbon meant what she thinks it did. I have not answered her either. You will see that I am beset on every side by people wanting the very words, and I begin to feel like a woman whose house has too many doors open to the street at once.

The town, meanwhile, has posted bills for a lecture on Buddhism at the Assembly Rooms, which the Echo treats with its usual sniff, and everyone is talking of the influenza coming round again with the cold, so that Mrs. Ashworth's parlour is not the only place where people court trouble against their better judgment.

Tell me plainly, as you always do — ought I let this man near me at all, or shut the door before he has properly got his foot across it?

I remain, wanting your plain sense more than usual,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 8: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
28 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

You ask for plain sense, and you shall have it without hedging. Shut the door on Mr. Strake. A man who tells you in the same breath that he does not mean to write you up and that he might is not offering candour but testing how much you will forgive in advance. I have known his sort at the Society's own meetings — Myers and Podmore quarrel like gentlemen over evidence, but the literary hangers-on who circle such quarrels care nothing for either side of the argument, only for a good page. Write to him, if you must write at all, that you have nothing further to add to what Mrs. Ashworth has already given the whole of Lansdown, which is, God knows, more than enough.

As for Miss Vane, I would have you delay any answer to her also, though for a different reason. Her letter, by your account, is tender rather than probing, and tenderness in these matters can be more dangerous than impertinence, for it disarms exactly where one ought to be most careful. Before you tell her what the ribbon meant, or confirm any particular of your own history, I should like to know a little more of her — where she lodged before Cheltenham, who first brought her into Mrs. Ashworth's drawing-room, whether she has any prior thread of acquaintance with your household. I do not ask this to torment you with suspicion, but because I would rather you answered her from certainty than from feeling, and I am better placed than you to make such inquiries quietly, without setting the whole of Lansdown talking afresh.

Give me a fortnight, and hold your pen still until I have written again. The fog is down thick over the river, and half London is coughing with the influenza already; it is no season for standing about with doors open to whoever knocks.

I remain, with more urgency than usual, your faithful friend,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 9: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
30 October 1891

My dear Dr. Pembroke,

You shall have your fortnight, and gladly, for I own I had no great heart to answer either of them, and your plain sense has, as usual, relieved me of a decision I was not fit to make alone. I shall write to Mr. Strake as you advise, and tell him I have nothing to add to Mrs. Ashworth's account — though I suspect a man of his stamp will read refusal as encouragement, and call it modesty rather than a closed door. We shall see. I shall hold my pen from Miss Vane also, though it costs me something, for her letter was so gently put that I feel almost unkind in my silence.

But I must say one thing plainly, since you have asked me to trust you with the inquiry itself. Go softly, Arthur. I do not doubt your discretion — I have leaned on it too long to doubt it now — but a household, even mine, has corners that were never meant for a physician's tidy questions, and old servants talk more freely to a stranger's kindness than they ever did to their mistress's face. I would rather Miss Vane's connections went unexamined altogether than have you turn up, in the running down of some idle hare, a matter that concerns no one but myself and is settled, whatever it was, years since. You will think this an odd anxiety in a woman who claims to want the truth of things. Perhaps it is. I ask it nonetheless.

The town has its Buddhism lecture to look forward to, which the Echo treats as it treats everything it does not understand, with a sniff and a leading article; and the influenza is spoken of everywhere, so that Mrs. Ashworth has already begun dosing herself and half of Lansdown against it. I keep indoors, as you would have me, and wait upon your fortnight with what patience I can find.

I remain, trusting you further than I sometimes wish to own,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 10: Miss Clara Vane to Mr. Julian Strake
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bath
1 November 1891

My dear Mr. Strake,

You will forgive my writing so directly, but I hear you have been asking questions about Mrs. Ashworth's Tuesday evening, and I would rather you had the truth of it from me than from her, since she has never in her life told a story the same way twice. I do not resent your curiosity — indeed I have always thought you the one person in Bath who looks at me without either contempt or credulity, which is a rarer thing than you would guess — but I confess I dread to see myself made into copy for some clever paragraph, set beside that dreadful mesmerist who has taken the Assembly Rooms this week and is drawing crowds to be laughed at. I am not a showman, Mr. Strake, whatever my enemies say, and I think you know it.

 What passed at Mrs. Ashworth's was not performance. I paid for it after, as I always do, in three days of headache and a sickness of spirit I cannot properly describe to anyone who has not felt it. Mrs. Marchmont understands this, I think, though she has not yet written to tell me so, and her silence weighs on me more than I like to own. If you speak with her, you might say — not as from me, but as your own observation — that I have been unwell since, and low, and that I would not for the world have caused her pain.

 The river is up again by the weir, and half the town coughing already before the real cold has come; I begin to think Bath in November is fit only for invalids and journalists, which perhaps makes us fellow-sufferers. Write to me, if you will, and tell me plainly what you mean to do with what you have heard. I would rather have your honesty than your admiration, though I own I should be glad of both.

I remain, with more frankness than is perhaps prudent, yours,
Clara Vane
