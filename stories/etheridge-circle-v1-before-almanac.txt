========================================================================
THE ETHERIDGE CIRCLE, 1891
========================================================================

On the evening of Monday the twelfth of October, 1891, a private séance was held
in the drawing-room of Mrs. Helena Marchmont at Cheltenham. Present were Mrs.
Marchmont, her companion, two neighbours of good family, and the medium, Miss
Clara Vane, lately of Bath. Dr. Arthur Pembroke of London, an old friend of the
late Mr. Marchmont, had declined his invitation and was not in the room.

After some minutes of the usual phenomena, Miss Vane, in trance, spoke in a voice
the company took to be that of Mrs. Marchmont's son, who had died some eighteen
months earlier. Among other things the voice said:

    "The blue ribbon was not burnt in Heaven."

Mrs. Marchmont was seen to go white and asked for the sitting to end. She has
since told nobody what the words meant to her. Within the week the matter had
reached Dr. Pembroke, and by some route nobody can quite trace, Mr. Julian Strake
of the London weeklies.

What follows is their correspondence.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 1: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
14 October 1891

My dear Dr. Pembroke,

I hope this finds you in good health, and that the business which kept you from Cheltenham last Tuesday evening was not of a troubling nature. I confess I missed your presence, though I suspect you were not sorry to be spared what you would call the spectacle.

I write now because I find I cannot settle the matter in my own mind, and I have long trusted your judgment above that of any other person left to me. Miss Vane conducted a sitting here, quiet and without ceremony, only Mr. Strake and myself and two others of my acquaintance. I will not pretend to you that nothing occurred. Something was said—a small thing, a trifling phrase—which struck me with a force I had not anticipated, and which I cannot yet explain to my own satisfaction.

You will say, I think, that coincidence and suggestion account for a great deal, and that a grieving woman is the readiest subject for both. I do not ask you to spare my feelings by pretending otherwise, for I know you too well to expect flattery from you, and I should not thank you for it if you offered it. But I ask you, as a man of science and as Edward's oldest friend, to tell me plainly what you believe such phenomena to be, without either mockery or the gentleness one reserves for the feeble-minded. I have had enough of both from other quarters.

I do not say I believe. I say only that I was moved, and that I distrust my own capacity to judge why. If there is a rational account of what passes in such rooms, I should rather hear it from you than remain in ignorance, however comforting that ignorance might otherwise prove.

Write to me honestly, Arthur. I think I can bear it.

I remain, with sincere regard, yours,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 2: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
16 October 1891

My dear Helena,

You do me the honour of asking for honesty rather than comfort, and I shall try to deserve it, though you must forgive me if the two prove, in places, indistinguishable.

I will not mock you. I have seen too many sound minds unsettled by grief to think unsettlement itself a mark of weakness, and you have never been a woman given to easy credulity. But you ask what I believe such phenomena to be, and I can only answer as a physician: that the mind under strain is a most accomplished author, and that it will furnish itself with exactly the sentence it most longs, or most dreads, to hear, and then present that sentence to us as though it had arrived from elsewhere. This is not dishonesty on your part. It is simply how grief works upon memory and expectation, and I have watched it work upon better-guarded minds than most.

But I cannot judge fairly what I have not heard, and you have told me remarkably little for a letter that asks so much of me. What, precisely, was the phrase? In whose voice was it given—Miss Vane's own, or through some other manner of utterance? Was it addressed to you directly, or overheard as it were sideways, as such things are so often arranged to be? And I should like very much to know whether Mr. Strake had, at any point before that evening, occasion to speak with you of Edward, of his habits, his turns of phrase, anything at all that a clever and attentive man might afterward return to you dressed as revelation.

I ask not to humiliate you, nor to flatter my own scepticism, but because a plain fact honestly examined will serve you better than any comfort I could invent. Tell me the words, Helena. I promise you my whole attention, and nothing less than my whole honesty.

I remain, with unaltered regard, yours,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 3: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
18 October 1891

My dear Arthur,

You ask for plain fact, and I will give you what I can, though you must permit me to withhold what is not yet mine to give even to myself, let alone to you.

The words were these: something concerning a blue ribbon. Spoken quietly, in Miss Vane's own voice, though altered, as though she listened for it as she said it rather than composed it. It was not addressed to me by name, nor delivered as a message with my name attached in the manner you clearly suspect such things are arranged. It came, as you put it, sideways, and I confess that is precisely what unsettled me, for had it been aimed at me directly I think I should have distrusted it at once, as you counsel.

As to Mr. Strake, I can tell you honestly that he never spoke to me of Edward before that evening beyond the ordinary courtesies one extends to a widow. He is clever, I grant you, and he is watchful, and I do not pretend he is without designs of some kind upon my good opinion. But cleverness is not omniscience, Arthur, and I cannot think even the most attentive man could have divined this particular thing from observation alone. I do not ask you to accept that as proof. I ask you only to consider it as a fact worth weighing, rather than dismissing on the strength of what cleverness is generally capable of.

As for Miss Vane, I will say this much in her defence: she asked nothing of me afterward, sought no further sitting, pressed for no fee beyond what was arranged before ever a word was spoken. If she is an impostor, she is an unusually patient one.

You say a plain fact honestly examined will serve me better than comfort. Very well. I have given you the fact. I ask now that you examine it as a fact, and not merely as a symptom of a widow's disordered longing, however gently you dress the diagnosis.

I remain, with unaltered trust, though not unaltered patience, yours,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 4: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
20 October 1891

My dear Helena,

You have given me a fact, as you promised, and I will not pretend it is a comfortable one, though not for the reason you suppose. A ribbon is a small thing to build a mystery upon, and yet small things are precisely what survive when larger ones are forgotten, which is why I must press you further rather than less.

Tell me what this ribbon was, in itself. Was it an object Edward kept, or one he gave, or one associated with some particular day or habit of his? Did it exist still, among his effects, or in your own keeping, where a servant, a visiting friend, even an idle guest turning over a drawer, might have seen it and spoken of it without malice, merely as gossip does? I do not ask this to diminish what you felt. I ask it because a detail that seems unaccountable often has a perfectly accountable history, once one is willing to trace it rather than marvel at it.

You defend Mr. Strake's ignorance of your late husband's habits, and perhaps you are right that he never spoke of Edward to you directly. But a patient man need not speak to learn. Servants talk, callers remark, and grief itself is generous with its confidences to anyone who appears to listen kindly. I would know also whether Miss Vane has ever, by any means, had access to your house, your letters, or your acquaintance, before that evening.

I will not insult you by suggesting you invent your own distress, Helena. I only ask you to let me test this one fact as rigorously as you would wish any physician to test a symptom, before either of us decides what it signifies. You said you could bear plainness from me. I am endeavouring, not always comfortably, to give you nothing else.

I remain, with unaltered regard, yours,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 5: Miss Clara Vane to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bath
22 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

I have thought of you every day since that evening, and of the stillness that came over the room when the ribbon was spoken of. I trust the days since have brought you some measure of peace, though I know such peace comes slowly, and never all at once, when the heart has been so recently opened.

I write, in part, because I feel I owe you an honesty that goes beyond the parlour and the usual courtesies. What passed through me that night was not, I think, mine to claim credit for — I was only the instrument, as a harp is instrument to the wind. I cannot always say whence such things come, only that they come, and that I have learned not to question too closely a gift I did not choose and cannot always govern. I tell you this because I would rather you thought me strange than false.

I confess I have thought too of Dr. Pembroke, though we have met but once, and coldly. I understand he takes an interest in such matters as mine — an interest, I fear, more suited to the dissecting-room than the drawing-room. I do not resent his scepticism; I have met it before, and shall again. But I own I should not like to think you were persuaded by his manner into doubting what your own heart told you that evening. You were there, dear Mrs. Marchmont, as I was. He was not.

I hope you will forgive my writing so plainly. It is only that I value your good opinion above almost any other, and would not have it shaken by a gentleman who studies grief as though it were a specimen under glass. I remain, as ever, entirely at your service, whenever you should wish to speak further of what came to pass — or simply to speak of other things, as friends do.

I remain, dear Mrs. Marchmont, your affectionate and faithful
Clara Vane


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 6: Mr. Julian Strake to Miss Clara Vane
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
24 October 1891

My dear Miss Vane,

I hope you will forgive a letter that presumes rather more upon acquaintance than three meetings in Mrs. Marchmont's drawing-room strictly license. But you struck me, on each of those occasions, as the one person in that circle who watched everyone else with more interest than she permitted anyone to take in her.

I will confess the immediate occasion of my writing. A neighbour of mine, who was present at the sitting last week, repeated to me a phrase said to have come through Mrs. Marchmont in that state of hers — I will not set it down here, for it loses everything in a plain telling, and gains a false solemnity from being written by a hand that did not hear it spoken. But it has stayed with me since, in the manner of a splinter one cannot quite work free.

I am considering — you must not repeat this, or only to Mrs. Marchmont if you judge it will not distress her — a sober essay on the present appetite for the unseen: Theosophy, spiritualism, the whole fashionable commerce with the dead. I have no wish to write anything cruel. I have rather less wish to write anything false, in either direction — to explain away what may be real, or to dignify what may be contrivance.

You, I think, see further into the business than you allow yourself to say. Will you tell me, in confidence, what you actually witnessed? Not what was reported to have been said, but what you saw of her face, her voice, the room. I ask as one who suspects you are the shrewdest sceptic present, and possibly the most moved.

I shall value whatever you are willing to give me, and shall use none of it without your leave.

I remain, with sincere regard, yours,
Julian Strake


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 7: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Miss Clara Vane
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
26 October 1891

My dear Miss Vane,

Your letter reached me at a moment when I had sore need of a friend's tenderness, and I read it twice before I could set it down. You need not fear that Dr. Pembroke's manner has shaken my regard for you. He is a good man, and Edward's oldest friend, but he examines grief as he would examine a fever, and I have told him so myself. I would far rather be thought strange in your company than safe in his.

You say you were only the instrument that evening, and I believe you mean it honestly. But you will forgive a widow's restless mind if I ask you one further question, not in Dr. Pembroke's spirit, but in my own. He presses me to know whether you had ever, by any means, occasion to come near my house or my acquaintance before that evening — whether through Mr. Strake's introductions, or through some caller of mine, or even, forgive the indelicacy, through servants' talk, which I know travels faster than any of us like to think. I do not ask because I doubt you. I ask because I wish to be able to answer him plainly, and to close the matter, rather than leave it open for his clinical curiosity to worry at indefinitely.

I hope you will not think me cold in asking it. I assure you the coldness is all borrowed from him, and none of it my own. Whatever your answer, I do not think it will alter what I felt that evening, which was not persuasion but recognition — though I could not tell even you, dear Miss Vane, of what it was I recognised.

Write to me soon, and speak, if you will, of other things also. I find I have grown greedy for your letters, as one grows greedy for any light in a dark season.

I remain, with true affection, yours,
Helena Marchmont


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 8: Miss Clara Vane to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Bath
28 October 1891

My dear Mrs. Marchmont,

I will answer you as plainly as you have asked, though I own the question cost me a moment's pain — not because it came from you, but because I understand now how far Dr. Pembroke's shadow has fallen across a friendship I had thought entirely our own. No, dear Mrs. Marchmont. I had never, before that evening, come near your house, nor exchanged a word with any caller of yours, nor with any servant of your household. Mr. Strake's introduction was the whole of my acquaintance with your circle, and that only across a drawing-room, in company, as you yourself witnessed. I have no acquaintance in Bath that touches your world at any point I can discover, search as I might, and I have searched, if only to be able to say this to you with a clear conscience.

I tell you this not merely to close Dr. Pembroke's inquiry, though I hope it may do that, but because I would not have even the smallest doubt sit between us unexamined. You have been generous with me in a way few have troubled to be, and I would rather lose every engagement in Bath than lose your good opinion through some indelicacy I had failed to answer honestly.

As to what you felt that evening — recognition, you called it, not persuasion — I think you have named it better than I could. Persuasion is the business of argument, of evidence marshalled and disputed, which is Dr. Pembroke's proper country and none of mine. Recognition is quieter; it asks no leave of the intellect. I have come to believe, the more I study these matters and their higher grammar, that such moments are not proofs at all in the vulgar sense, but a kind of remembering the soul does when it is shown its own truth. That you should doubt the messenger is natural. That you should doubt what you yourself recognised seems to me a harder and less necessary grief to bear.

Write to me of other things too, as you say. I find I have grown as greedy for your letters as you are for mine.

I remain, with true and grateful affection, yours,
Clara Vane


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 9: Dr. Arthur Pembroke to Mrs. Helena Marchmont
------------------------------------------------------------------------

London
30 October 1891

My dear Helena,

I have had no answer from you in ten days, and I find I mind it more than a physician ought to mind the silence of a patient he has merely advised. I hope it means only that you have been occupied, and not that my last letter pressed too near some tenderness you had rather keep whole than examined.

I have not been idle in the meantime. I took the liberty—forgive me, for I know you did not ask it of me—of making certain quiet inquiries as to Miss Vane's history before she came to Cheltenham. I learn that she was for some two years companion to a family in Sussex, and that the connexion ended abruptly, though no one I spoke with could or would say why. It is not proof of anything, Helena. I am careful to tell you so before you can accuse me of building a case on gossip, as I have accused Mr. Strake of doing worse. But a woman who has lived intimately in other people's houses learns other people's small secrets whether she seeks them or no, and I think it a fact worth setting beside your ribbon.

As to that ribbon itself—I confess I have turned the word over more than I like to admit, and I cannot yet say whether it stirs in me some memory of Edward's own, or whether I am simply a man grown suspicious of his own recollections from too much dwelling on other people's. You will think that a strange thing for your rational physician to confess. Perhaps it is. I set it down only because I promised you plainness and find I owe you a little of my own uncertainty along with my scrutiny of yours.

I own, too, that I do not like the ease with which Mr. Strake seems to have made himself necessary to your evenings. That may be nothing more than an old man's jealousy of a livelier tongue than his own. Write to me, Helena. Even to tell me I am wrong.

I remain, with unaltered and perhaps too anxious regard, yours,
Arthur Pembroke


------------------------------------------------------------------------
Letter 10: Mrs. Helena Marchmont to Dr. Arthur Pembroke
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cheltenham
1 November 1891

My dear Arthur,

I have your letter, and I will own it disturbed my composure more than any other you have sent me. You say you took a liberty in inquiring after Miss Vane's history, and you are right to call it a liberty, for it was taken without my leave and, I think, without much regard for what it might cost her to have her past turned over by strangers on my behalf. She came to me in good faith, Arthur, and I will not have her paid for that faith with the sort of quiet inquiry one makes of a servant suspected of theft. If you wished to know whether she had ever had access to my house or my acquaintance before that evening, you had only to ask me to ask her, as I did. She tells me, plainly and without evasion, that she had none — no acquaintance with any caller of mine, no word with any servant, nothing beyond the single introduction Mr. Strake gave her across a drawing-room, in company, as I myself witnessed. I believe her. I ask you to let that answer close the question, rather than send you further afield into a stranger's misfortunes in Sussex, which, whatever they were, are surely her own to keep or disclose as she chooses, and no business of a physician who has never met the family concerned.

As for the ribbon, and what it stirs in you — I will not press you to resolve what you yourself confess is unresolved. You said you could not tell whether it touched some memory of Edward's own or merely your own suspicious habit of mind. I think, Arthur, you must be content to leave it there a while, as I must leave a great many things there, whether I like it or no.

I do not think you jealous of Mr. Strake, only tired, and perhaps a little wounded that I have found comfort where you offer chiefly correction. I do not despise your correction. I only ask that it stop short of my friends.

I remain, with real but wearied affection, yours,
Helena Marchmont
