AN EVENING AT MRS. ASHWORTH'S

The Etheridge Circle, a fortnight on.

FADE IN:

INT. MRS. ASHWORTH'S DRAWING-ROOM, LANSDOWN, CHELTENHAM - NIGHT

The drawing-room of Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth's house in Lansdown,
Cheltenham. An evening in the last week of October, 1891, a
fortnight after the sitting at which Miss Vane, in trance,
spoke of a blue ribbon. Gas and candles; a good fire; a
sideboard with champagne cup, claret cup, sherry, ices and
sandwiches; an ottoman; a tall window on to the dark garden;
the door to the hall, where the guests are received. Mrs.
Ashworth has asked the same people again, "so that we may all
be comfortable together." They have all known one another for
years, and are fond of one another, which is what makes the
evening dangerous. Upstairs, on the second floor, lives her
late husband's brother, the Reverend Ambrose Ashworth,
formerly a chaplain in Bengal, who does not come down.

MRS. MARCHMONT and DR. PEMBROKE stand by the fire, a little
apart from the rest, each holding a glass of cup neither has
touched. Across the room MISS VANE examines the sideboard, MR.
STRAKE the dark garden, and MRS. ASHWORTH, in the doorway, the
hall.

At the fire: MRS. MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (warms her hands at the fire, not
                quite looking at him)
          Arthur, how good of you to come.
          Lavinia does love to gather us all in
          one room and watch what happens.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (studies her face a moment too
                long)
          She gathers us like specimens under
          glass, and I confess I have never yet
          seen what she hopes will happen. You
          look well, Helena — better than in
          the spring, I think.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (glances at him, then back to the
                flames)
          Better? You are kind to say so, though
          I suspect you are counting the grey
          hairs and being generous about it. I
          manage, Arthur. One does.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (watches her hands rather than her
                face)
          One does, yes — though 'managing' is
          a word I distrust in a patient. You
          are sleeping? Eating? Do not humour me
          with vague reassurances, Helena, I
          know your vague reassurances too well.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (lifts her chin slightly, a small
                defiance)
          I sleep as well as a woman of my age
          has any right to expect, and I eat
          enough to keep Cook from despairing of
          me. There, Arthur — will that
          satisfy your professional conscience?

MRS. ASHWORTH crosses the room and joins them at the fire.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (joins them at the fire, cup in
                hand)
          There you both are! Dear Helena, you
          know Dr. Pembroke, of course —
          though perhaps not that he has taken
          rooms quite near you now, on Suffolk
          Square, since his poor practice in
          London failed him.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (does not look at either of them,
                watches the fire)
          Suffolk Square — how near indeed.
          Strange, is it not, how a person may
          come to stand almost at one's elbow
          without one's ever quite marking the
          moment it happened.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (turns from the fire toward Dr.
                Pembroke, smiling)
          Dr. Pembroke, you have scarcely
          touched your cup — let me fetch you
          something warmer, or an ice, whichever
          you fancy. You must not stand about
          looking clever, it frightens the
          younger guests.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (declines the offered cup with a
                small gesture)
          Failed is Lavinia's word, not mine —
          I closed the practice by choice, to
          pursue less lucrative curiosities. As
          for Suffolk Square, I took rooms there
          for the quiet. Not that where I lodge
          is any concern of anyone's.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (presses a small dish of ices
                toward him, beaming)
          There now, quiet is just the thing
          after London — though I daresay
          Suffolk Square is dreadfully draughty
          in this wind. Do have an ice, Dr.
          Pembroke, the lemon ones are
          excellent.

MISS VANE crosses the room and joins them at the fire.

                      MISS VANE
                (joins the fire, smiling, hands
                lightly clasped before her)
          Mrs. Ashworth, the room is quite
          transformed tonight—the candlelight
          suits your new curtains beautifully.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (turns beaming from Miss Vane to
                Dr. Pembroke, ices still in hand)
          How kind of you, my dear — but Dr.
          Pembroke, you must know Miss Vane
          properly. My discovery! She kept
          lodgings above the chemist's in
          Leckhampton before dear Helena took
          her up.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (regards Miss Vane with polite,
                measuring attention)
          Above the chemist's — a useful
          vantage, that, for observing which
          draughts sell best. Miss Vane, I
          understand you have some reputation as
          a sensitive. I confess I am the
          dullest sort of audience for such
          things.

                      MISS VANE
                (meets his gaze steadily, chin
                lifted just slightly)
          Dullest audiences make the fairest
          judges, Dr. Pembroke — I have no
          wish to persuade you of anything you
          are not already inclined to see for
          yourself.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (inclines his head slightly, still
                measuring her)
          A fair answer — fairer than I
          expected. Very well, Miss Vane: I
          shall watch, and judge, and say
          nothing until I have grounds. That is
          all I ever promise anyone.

MR. STRAKE crosses the room and joins them at the fire.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (warms hands at the fire, glancing
                round the circle)
          Lavinia, my dear, this fire is a
          masterpiece of stagecraft—too
          handsome to be accidental. Who
          arranged it, you or Providence?

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (beams at Mr. Strake, cup warming
                her hands)
          Providence may lay the coals, Julian,
          but it is Parker who tends them — I
          take no credit beyond ringing the
          bell. You are too generous by half, as
          always.

MRS. ASHWORTH goes out. A pause; the talk falters. The company
drifts toward the fire. She returns leading the REVEREND
AMBROSE ASHWORTH by the arm: seventy, a black coat too large
for him, his hat held in both hands.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (draws Ambrose into the circle by
                the sleeve, takes his hat)
          Here is my brother Ambrose, the
          Reverend Ashworth — vicar of a
          Prestbury parish so poor it scarce
          keeps his roof mended. Think, Ambrose,
          and say something clever to Dr.
          Pembroke.

                      AMBROSE
                (He settles by the fire, hands
                folded, eyes fixed on the flames)
          The mean temperature at Barrackpore in
          the month of May being considerably in
          excess of that which the European
          constitution was designed by
          Providence to sustain, the blood
          thickens, yes, thickens as the
          Thirty-Nine Articles thicken in the
          mouth of a young curate who has not
          digested Article Nine on Original Sin
          nor the leg-break bowled to him by
          Fitzgerald in seventy-one, a maiden
          over, a maiden, and the soul likewise
          is bowled a maiden over by the
          Adversary unless girded, the pith
          helmet being no defence against that
          particular delivery, no defence at
          all, the 4.15 from Cheltenham calling
          at Ashchurch, Bredon, and thence to
          Birmingham New Street, though the
          timetable of eternity has no Bredon in
          it, no calling anywhere, only arrival,
          or so the Mahatmas would have it, the
          astral body travelling first class
          while the physical remains,
          sun-struck, in the compartment marked
          Bengal Civil Service, retired, unfit
          for further duty, the barometer
          falling, the barometer falling as it
          fell before the Mutiny, before the
          punkah stopped, before the register of
          baptisms was eaten by white ants at
          Dinapore, eaten entire, Genesis to
          Revelation, the wicket-keeper standing
          back for the fast bowler as the
          Athanasian Creed stands back from the
          Nicene, quicunque vult, quicunque
          vult, whosoever will be saved,
          whosoever will be saved, it is a fine
          evening for the time of year, a fine
          evening, the glass steady, steady as
          the pulse of a man of seventy who has
          seen the ganges and the thermometer
          and the fall of wickets and the fall
          of man, whosoever will, whosoever
          will, the fire wants making up, the
          fire, the fire.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (touches the mantel, a little
                unsteady)
          He says the fire wants making up —
          the very words, Miss Vane, as I stood
          here thinking of nothing else. One
          cannot always call such things mere
          chance.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (steps toward Ambrose, then checks
                himself, turning back)
          That is not prophecy, Helena, that is
          a mind unmoored — thickened blood, a
          fever perhaps, old sunstroke turning
          on him. The fire is merely what a man
          before a fire says. Forgive me, this
          troubles me more than I can easily
          disguise.

                      MISS VANE
                (watches Ambrose's face, no longer
                smiling)
          Do not apologize, Doctor — I think
          you are quite right, and it frightens
          me more than I can say to hear how
          alike a fevered mind and a listening
          one can sound.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (does not look at the fire,
                watches Ambrose's folded hands)
          Lavinia — forgive me — has he been
          like this long? That is not a man
          performing confusion, that is a man
          drowning in his own mind, and I find I
          cannot make light of it.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (presses his hat into his hands,
                draws him firmly by the arm toward
                the door)
          Forgive us — poor Ambrose tires so
          early these days, he must have his
          rest before the fire wearies him
          further. Come, Ambrose, your hat —
          we shall find you a quiet chair.

MRS. ASHWORTH drifts away toward the window.

MRS. ASHWORTH leads AMBROSE out. The door closes. Nobody
speaks for a moment.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (watches the door where Ambrose
                was led out)
          Poor man—that was no performance,
          whatever else it was. Lavinia has
          borne it alone too long, I think. We
          must be kinder to her than we have
          been tonight.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (watches the door, then turns back
                to the fire)
          Kinder, yes — and practical. Lavinia
          should not manage this alone; there
          are physicians in Cheltenham who
          understand such conditions better than
          curates or clergymen's sisters.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (turns from the door, folding her
                hands together)
          Yes—and I shall call on her tomorrow
          myself, quietly, before she can refuse
          the offer. She has sat with grief
          before; she ought not sit with this
          alone either.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (warms his hands at the fire,
                voice low and practical)
          Quietly, before you call — I might
          look in on him myself, if Lavinia
          would permit it. Not as a specialist,
          only as an old friend who happens to
          carry a black bag.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (looks at him with real gratitude,
                some old grief showing)
          Thank you, Arthur — that is kindly
          done, and I shall not pretend I am not
          relieved to hear it. You were always
          the one who knew what to do when the
          rest of us only wrung our hands.

MRS. ASHWORTH crosses the room and joins them at the fire.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (returns to the fire, smoothing
                her skirts, ices forgotten)
          There, he is quite settled with Parker
          and some beef tea — himself again in
          no time, I promise you. Now, Clara,
          you must know Julian properly: he
          clerks for Mr. Bellamy the solicitor
          and lodges in Bayshill Terrace, though
          you would never guess it from that
          coat.

                      MISS VANE
                (smiles faintly at Julian, still
                shaken by the reverend's turn)
          Mr. Strake and I are already
          acquainted, Mrs. Ashworth — though I
          confess I still cannot guess a clerk
          from that coat either.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (holds out the cup with both
                hands, voice a touch unsteady)
          You are all so good to be troubled for
          him — he was a great scholar once,
          and sunstroke took more from him than
          his hearing took from me. Come, warm
          cup, all of you, before the ices melt
          entirely.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (takes the cup from her, holds her
                hand a moment longer than needed)
          Warm cup, then, and gladly — you've
          had trouble enough for one evening,
          Lavinia, without playing hostess to my
          sentiment on top of it.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (colours slightly, does not
                withdraw her hand at once)
          Oh — you dear man. There, now,
          you'll quite undo me if you go on so
          kindly. Drink your cup before it's
          stone cold, Julian, and no more
          sentiment tonight.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (drinks, smiling, though his eyes
                flick once toward the door)
          No more sentiment, then — only the
          cup, which I promise to praise
          honestly: it wants nutmeg, Lavinia,
          and you know it.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (turns from Julian, drawing Clara
                gently toward the doctor)
          Nutmeg, always nutmeg — you are
          quite right, Julian, and I shall scold
          Parker directly. Now, Clara, you must
          not go without knowing Dr. Pembroke
          properly — he has his rooms in
          Suffolk Square, quite the cleverest
          physician in Cheltenham.

                      MISS VANE
                (inclines her head to him, hands
                folded before her)
          I am glad to know you properly, Dr.
          Pembroke — and gladder still for
          what you mean to do for Mrs. Ashworth.
          That was no small kindness, whatever
          else stands between us.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (inclines his head slightly, still
                warming his hands at the fire)
          You are kind to say so, Miss Vane,
          though I did nothing but offer what
          any sensible neighbour would. Medicine
          is mostly patience dressed up in
          Latin.

                      MISS VANE
                (glances toward the door where the
                reverend was led out)
          Patience dressed as Latin — I think
          that describes half the comfort in
          this room tonight, Doctor, mine
          included, though mine wears different
          dress.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (does not take the cup Mrs.
                Ashworth offers, shakes his head
                slightly)
          Different dress indeed, Miss Vane —
          I confess I have never quite decided
          whether yours comforts or merely
          resembles comfort. A physician's habit
          of doubt, nothing more.

The candles gutter. MRS. MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE, MISS VANE,
MR. STRAKE are still there when MRS. ASHWORTH turns down the
gas.

FADE OUT.
