AN EVENING AT MRS. ASHWORTH'S

The Etheridge Circle, a fortnight on.

The drawing-room of Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth's house in Lansdown,
Cheltenham. An evening in the last week of October, 1891, a
fortnight after the sitting at which Miss Vane, in trance,
spoke of a blue ribbon. Gas and candles; a good fire; a
sideboard with champagne cup, claret cup, sherry, ices and
sandwiches; an ottoman; a tall window on to the dark garden;
the door to the hall, where the guests are received. Mrs.
Ashworth has asked the same people again, "so that we may all
be comfortable together." They have all known one another for
years, and are fond of one another, which is what makes the
evening dangerous. Upstairs, on the second floor, lives her
late husband's brother, the Reverend Ambrose Ashworth,
formerly a chaplain in Bengal, who does not come down.

FADE IN:

SCENE 1

INT. THE HALL, MRS. ASHWORTH'S HOUSE - NIGHT, CONTINUOUS

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth receives Mrs. Marchmont and
Dr. Pembroke in the hall, delighted to have got them both
under her roof again.]

MRS. ASHWORTH, MRS. MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE, in the hall.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (takes both their coats at once,
                beaming)
          Dr. Pembroke! Only fancy — Helena,
          you remember Dr. Pembroke, just down
          from London, where he has the most
          fashionable practice in Wimpole
          Street. Dr. Pembroke, Mrs. Marchmont,
          my dearest neighbour of eleven years!

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (extends her hand to Dr. Pembroke,
                smiling)
          Arthur and I have been acquainted
          these twenty years, Lavinia — though
          I thank you for the introduction all
          the same.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (bustles them toward the
                drawing-room, coats over her arm)
          Twenty years! Well, there is nothing
          like an old friendship warmed over —
          do go through, both of you, the fire
          is lit and there is sherry going
          round.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (watches Lavinia disappear with
                the coats, then turns to him)
          You look well, Arthur, though London
          has thinned you somewhat. I confess I
          have missed being lectured at by
          someone who does not humour me.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (removes his gloves slowly,
                watching her face)
          No, I never humour anyone — least of
          all myself. I did wonder, not that it
          is any concern of mine, whether
          Cheltenham has kept you in better
          company than London has kept me.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (folds her hands, meets his eyes
                steadily)
          Better company, perhaps, but no less
          need of correction. Cheltenham
          indulges me shamefully, Arthur —
          everyone here is so very careful of my
          feelings. It grows wearisome.

MISS VANE comes in.

                      MISS VANE
                (smiles and glances briefly at Dr.
                Pembroke before turning back)
          How good of you to have us, Mrs.
          Ashworth—the hall is quite
          transformed since Tuesday. Cheltenham
          grows lovelier each time I come.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (draws Miss Vane forward by the
                arm toward Dr. Pembroke)
          Miss Vane, you must meet Dr. Pembroke
          — he has come down specially from
          London, where he keeps rooms in
          Wimpole Street, and Miss Vane, my
          dear, lives quite alone in Suffolk
          Square since her father died.

                      MISS VANE
                (extends her hand with practiced
                composure, chin slightly lifted)
          Dr. Pembroke. I have heard your name
          mentioned — Wimpole Street sounds a
          great distance from us. I hope
          Cheltenham does not disappoint after
          London.

Nobody answers.

                                                  CUT TO:

SCENE 2

INT. THE LIBRARY, MRS. ASHWORTH'S HOUSE - NIGHT, MOMENTS LATER

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth brings Mr. Strake and Miss
Vane together in the library, introducing each with the very
fact the other would least wish known.]

MRS. ASHWORTH, MR. STRAKE, MISS VANE, in the library.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (draws them together by the
                elbows, beaming between the two)
          Miss Vane, you must know Mr. Strake
          properly — our own remarkable
          discovery, who found the ribbon! And
          Mr. Strake, this is the clever man who
          writes it all up for the London
          papers.

                      MISS VANE
                (turns to Strake with a small,
                knowing smile)
          Mr. Strake and I are old
          acquaintances, Mrs. Ashworth, though
          he flatters me by writing me up so
          kindly. I hope he means to be gentle
          with us tonight.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (bustles off toward the
                drawing-room, glancing back once,
                beaming)
          Old acquaintances, better still —
          then you shall have no need of me to
          smooth things along! I must see to the
          ices before they melt. (pats Miss
          Vane's arm fondly)

MRS. ASHWORTH goes out, toward the parlour.

                      MISS VANE
                (tilts her head, studying him with
                faint amusement)
          Alone at last, Mr. Strake. Tell me
          plainly — do you come tonight to
          report a miracle, or to catch me out
          in one? I should like to know which
          pen you carry.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (glances toward the hearth, then
                back to her, smiling)
          Neither pen, Miss Vane — tonight I
          carry only my eyes, and they've
          already found something to admire:
          that fire, banked just so, throwing
          exactly the light a room wants for
          confidences. A small perfection.

                      MISS VANE
                (watches him over the rim of her
                glass, unhurried)
          A small perfection you suspect I
          arranged myself, Mr. Strake? I confess
          I have never yet learned to bank a
          fire—only to sit near one
          gratefully, like anybody else.

MRS. MARCHMONT comes in.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (turns slightly, placing herself
                nearer Miss Vane)
          Craftsmanship, Mr. Strake? You make it
          sound as though Miss Vane had built a
          cabinet. I assure you, what passed at
          that table was neither built nor
          performed — whatever your London
          readers may prefer to believe.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (inclines his head toward her,
                disarming, a little rueful)
          No, no—I meant no slight, Mrs.
          Marchmont, only that a well-made room
          deserves its due, as does a well-made
          evening. You mistake admiration for
          accusation; I do it constantly, and it
          costs me friends.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (glances at Miss Vane, then holds
                Strake's gaze steadily)
          Friends who mistake admiration for
          accusation as often as you claim, Mr.
          Strake, generally mean rather more by
          the admiration than they let show.
          Miss Vane deserves your good opinion
          honestly given, not dressed as a
          compliment with a blade in it.

MISS VANE sways, hand at her brow. "Forgive me — I feel him
near just now, something blue, a ribbon —" MRS. MARCHMONT
goes white and says nothing.

                                                  CUT TO:

SCENE 3

INT. THE LIBRARY, MRS. ASHWORTH'S HOUSE - NIGHT, MOMENTS LATER

[Director's note: Dr. Pembroke follows Mrs. Marchmont into the
library, where he means to talk sense into her and instead
talks himself into a corner.]

DR. PEMBROKE, MRS. MARCHMONT, in the library.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (draws a chair nearer the hearth
                for her)
          Sit by the fire a moment, Helena. It
          is not your nerves I doubt, but this
          Miss Vane — I should like to know
          what she has been telling you, and why
          it troubles you so.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (sits, smoothing her skirts, not
                quite meeting his eyes)
          Troubles me? No, Arthur — it
          interests me, which is not at all the
          same thing, whatever you and your
          Wimpole Street colleagues may choose
          to call it. Sit, if you like, but do
          not diagnose me before I have even
          taken tea.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (leans back from the fire, hands
                folded, watching her steadily)
          I do not diagnose out of jealousy,
          Helena — that is a symptom, a morbid
          excess of self-regard mistaking itself
          for love. I am trained to recognise it
          precisely because I am not subject to
          it.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (looks into the fire, not at him)
          Not jealousy, Arthur — call it what
          you like, but do not call it that. I
          have buried a husband and a son; I
          have very little self left to regard,
          morbidly or otherwise.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (leans forward, takes her hand
                briefly, then releases it)
          That was thoughtless of me. I did not
          mean to bring the boy into it. Forgive
          me, Helena — grief does not require
          my correction, only my silence, now
          and then.

MR. STRAKE comes in.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (inclines his head, smiling,
                though his eyes stay watchful)
          Fair hit, Mrs. Marchmont — and
          beautifully delivered, if I may say
          so: not a syllable wasted, the timing
          exact. You'd have made a formidable
          advocate. I meant tribute, though I
          grant tribute and accusation wear the
          same coat, on me.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (turns her hand over where his had
                lain, as if measuring it)
          There is nothing to forgive, Arthur.
          You have never once flinched from
          saying his name, which is more than
          most of Cheltenham has managed in a
          twelvemonth.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (does not rise; regards Strake
                over his spectacles)
          Strake. We had thought ourselves quite
          private. Did the drawing-room prove
          insufficiently amusing, or is it
          merely that you cannot resist an
          unattended lady and a low fire?

                      MR. STRAKE
                (glances between them, feigning
                easy contrition)
          Caught red-handed, Pembroke — I came
          only for the fire, and stumbled on
          something warmer still. Forgive me;
          I'll not pretend I didn't hear that
          name pass between you.

MRS. MARCHMONT rises abruptly, gathering her shawl; PEMBROKE
says nothing further, watching STRAKE, who does not look away.

                                                  CUT TO:

SCENE 4

INT. THE LIBRARY, MRS. ASHWORTH'S HOUSE - NIGHT, LATER

[Director's note: Miss Vane retreats to the library to recover
her composure, but Dr. Pembroke and Mr. Strake follow her,
each for his own reason.]

MISS VANE, MR. STRAKE, DR. PEMBROKE, in the library.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (sets down his sherry, untouched,
                and turns fully toward her)
          Miss Vane — since we are private,
          may I ask you plainly about the
          sitting a fortnight ago. Was the room
          dark throughout, or only at the moment
          the voice was heard?

                      MISS VANE
                (shivers slightly, drawing her
                shawl closer about her shoulders)
          The room was lit as it always is at
          first—only... forgive me, Dr.
          Pembroke, I feel a strange chill just
          now, speaking of it. As though someone
          had opened a window behind me.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (watches her hands, not her face,
                as she answers)
          A chill is easily explained, Miss Vane
          — a draught under the door, a change
          in the fire's draw. Was there in fact
          a window near you, or only the
          sensation of one?

                      MISS VANE
                (meets his gaze steadily, folding
                her hands in her lap)
          There was no window, Dr.
          Pembroke—only the sensation, as I
          said. I do not pretend to explain it
          scientifically; I only tell you what I
          felt. Perhaps that troubles you more
          than a window would.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (removes his spectacles, polishes
                them slowly, watching her)
          It troubles me only in the sense that
          unexplained sensations invite
          unexamined conclusions, Miss Vane. A
          chill felt is a fact of the body; a
          chill attributed to spirit is a leap
          of the mind. I merely ask which step
          you took.

                      MISS VANE
                (sways slightly, pressing gloved
                fingers to her temple)
          Forgive me, Dr. Pembroke—the room
          seems to press in upon me quite
          suddenly. Might I beg a chair, and
          perhaps a little cup? I find I cannot
          stand just now.

MRS. ASHWORTH comes in.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (sweeps in with a tray, offering
                it round eagerly)
          Ices, all of you — and Mr. Strake,
          do have Miss Vane tell the doctor
          about her father's house, since he
          lodged just where the doctor takes his
          rooms in Wimpole Street once, did you
          not, Mr. Strake?

                      MR. STRAKE
                (takes an ice from the tray,
                offers it to her with a bow)
          Let me fetch it, Miss Vane — you've
          earned it twice over. That was
          masterly, if I may say so: you gave
          the doctor nothing he could pin down,
          and did it so gracefully he'll thank
          you for the trouble.

                      MISS VANE
                (accepts the ice, glancing
                gratefully toward Mrs. Ashworth's
                retreating figure)
          Masterly is unkind, Mr. Strake — I
          only ever say what I feel. Thank you
          for the ice; it's very good of you.
          Mrs. Ashworth spoils us all shamefully
          tonight.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (settles against the shelves, arms
                folded, watching Pembroke rather
                than Clara)
          Pembroke, let the poor woman eat her
          ice in peace — you'll have her
          diagnosing your pulse next. Miss Vane,
          pay him no mind; he cross-examines the
          weather when there's no one else to
          hand.

MRS. ASHWORTH (turning back at the door) adds brightly that
she showed Miss Vane the escritoire — dear Helena's letters
and all — the very afternoon before the sitting; then
falters, silent.

                                                  CUT TO:

SCENE 5

INT. THE DRAWING-ROOM, MRS. ASHWORTH'S HOUSE - NIGHT, CONTINUOUS

[Director's note: Dr. Pembroke, Mrs. Marchmont, Mrs. Ashworth
and Miss Vane remain by the fire in the drawing-room, where
the fact of the escritoire will not be let alone, and the
evening tips toward its close.]

MRS. ASHWORTH, MRS. MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE, MISS VANE, in the
drawing-room.

MRS. ASHWORTH goes out. A pause. She returns leading the
REVEREND AMBROSE ASHWORTH by the arm: seventy, a black coat
too large for him, his hat held in both hands.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (takes his hat and gloves,
                straightens his collar with two
                fingers)
          Ambrose, there you are — do come and
          be looked at. Helena, Dr. Pembroke,
          Miss Vane, my brother Ambrose, who has
          promised to be very quiet and very
          good tonight.

                      AMBROSE
                (He straightens, blinking at the
                lamplight, hands folded as though
                at prayer)
          The mind, being an organ, or not an
          organ, but a faculty, situate perhaps
          in the pineal gland which the Hindoo
          priests call the third eye, though
          Article Nine says original sin doth
          stand not in the following of Adam,
          the wicket was slow at Lord's in
          seventy-four, run out by a yard, clean
          run out, the up train from Paddington
          departeth eleven forty and arriveth
          Cheltenham Spa four minutes past, or
          was it the down train, the barometer
          falling, glass at twenty-nine and
          dropping, monsoon somewhere over
          Bengal, the punkah-wallah asleep at
          his rope, the collector's bungalow,
          tiffin at one, the liver being
          congested by the sun as mine was
          congested, they said congested, Dr
          Hutton said congested, justification
          by faith only is a most wholesome
          doctrine, Article Eleven, and yet the
          byes were run twice, three byes, no
          balls do not count toward the total,
          and Madame Blavatsky says the astral
          body travels while the gross body
          sleeps, sleeps as India slept under
          the punkah, the thermometer one
          hundred and four in the shade, no
          shade, no shade at all only the glare
          off the Hooghly, the Hooghly is not
          the Ganges though the natives say it
          is holy all the same, holy, wholesome,
          Article Eleven, wholesome doctrine,
          the wicket-keeper stumped him,
          stumped, the sun stumped me, the 4.10
          from Gloucester calls at Cheltenham,
          calls at Cheltenham, weather set fair,
          glass rising, the Bishop of Calcutta
          preached upon the Articles, upon the
          third eye, upon the wicket, wholesome
          doctrine, wholesome, calls at
          Cheltenham, stumped, stumped,
          wholesome, wholesome, Cheltenham,
          Cheltenham, Cheltenham.

                      MRS. MARCHMONT
                (rises quickly, one hand pressed
                briefly to her collarbone)
          Lavinia — perhaps he ought to sit.
          That is not quietness, whatever else
          it is; a chair, some water, and less
          lamplight, I think, would serve him
          better than an audience.

                      DR. PEMBROKE
                (crosses quickly to Ambrose, takes
                his wrist to find the pulse)
          Helena is right — sit him down, away
          from the lamps, and loosen his collar.
          That is not foolishness, Mrs.
          Ashworth, that is a mind in real
          distress. Let me look at him.

                      MISS VANE
                (watches Ambrose with something
                like real fear, not performing it)
          He spoke of the astral body, Mrs.
          Marchmont—Madame Blavatsky's own
          words, from my own mouth once. I do
          not know what to make of that.

                      MRS. ASHWORTH
                (takes Ambrose firmly by the arm,
                retrieves his hat from the stand)
          No, no, Dr. Pembroke is quite right
          — too much excitement for one
          evening, that is all. Come, Ambrose,
          the air in the hall will do you a
          world of good.

MRS. ASHWORTH leads AMBROSE out. The door closes. Nobody
speaks for a moment.

MR. STRAKE comes in.

                      MR. STRAKE
                (glances at Mrs. Marchmont's
                stillness, then steps between her
                and Pembroke)
          Letters, Pembroke? You'll have Miss
          Vane's whole correspondence in
          evidence next. Some things a woman
          writes in grief were never meant for a
          drawing-room's cross-examination —
          not even yours.

No one answers STRAKE. MRS. ASHWORTH steadies AMBROSE toward
the door; HELENA reaches for her gloves; PEMBROKE says nothing
at all.

                                                  CUT TO:

FADE OUT.
