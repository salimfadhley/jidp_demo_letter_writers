AN EVENING AT MRS. ASHWORTH'S

A play in five scenes. The Etheridge Circle, a fortnight on.

PERSONS OF THE PLAY
    MRS. ASHWORTH, Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth
    MRS. MARCHMONT, Mrs. Helena Marchmont
    DR. PEMBROKE, Dr. Arthur Pembroke
    MISS VANE, Miss Clara Vane
    MR. STRAKE, Mr. Julian Strake
    AMBROSE, The Reverend Ambrose Ashworth
    CRUTTWELL, Mr. Barnabas Cruttwell

Rooms of Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth's house in Lansdown, Cheltenham. An
evening in the last week of October, 1891, a fortnight after the sitting
at which Miss Vane, in trance, spoke of a blue ribbon. Each scene is
played in one room, furnished lightly: a sideboard with champagne cup,
claret cup, sherry, ices and sandwiches; a few chairs nobody uses for
long; doors right and left. The fireplace and the window are downstage,
in the fourth wall, so that a character who warms her hands or looks
into the garden looks out at the audience. Mrs. Ashworth has asked the
same people again, "so that we may all be comfortable together." They
have all known one another for years, and are fond of one another, which
is what makes the evening dangerous. Upstairs lives her late husband's
brother, the Reverend Ambrose Ashworth, formerly a chaplain in Bengal,
who does not come down, and with him the man she pays to manage him, Mr.
Barnabas Cruttwell, lately of the asylum at Gloucester.

SCENE ONE

The drawing-room. Continuous. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MR. STRAKE,
MISS VANE.

[Director's note: Mr. Strake regales Mrs. Ashworth and Miss Vane in the
drawing-room with an account of a Theosophical sitting he attended in
London, admiring its craft rather more than its truth.]

        [Spotlight: MR. STRAKE]

MR. STRAKE. Lavinia, you've done it again—this room looks entirely
    unrehearsed, and I know for a fact you spent an hour arranging those
    lilies to seem as though no one had touched them at all.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (turns toward Miss Vane, drawing her into the circle)
    Julian, you wicked flatterer—caught out entirely! Now come, you must
    tell Clara of the Theosophical rooms before another soul arrives and
    steals you from me. She is dying to hear it, are you not, my dear?

MR. STRAKE. Willingly—though I confess, Lavinia's introduction was
    itself a small masterpiece of stagecraft. Clara, you listen so
    beautifully that I begin to suspect you could teach the Mahatmas a thing
    or two about presentation.

MISS VANE. You flatter me, Mr. Strake, but I am truly curious—was it a
    voice that spoke, or a sign only? I find I never tire of hearing how it
    comes to others.

MR. STRAKE. (He watches her face rather than Lavinia's, gauging what she
    makes of it) A voice—low, quite deliberate, naming things it could not
    have known. No trumpets, no drapery tricks. I sat close enough to swear
    to the dark and the stillness, if nothing else.

MISS VANE. The stillness matters more than people credit, I think. Was
    it truly dark, Mr. Strake, or only dim enough to seem so afterward, in
    the telling?

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

        (Enter DR. PEMBROKE.)

DR. PEMBROKE. Lavinia, you collect remarkable people the way other women
    collect china. Tell me—do you keep them for their beauty, or for what
    they might break?

MRS. ASHWORTH. (turns from Dr. Pembroke to Miss Vane, drawing them
    together with a wave of her fan) For their beauty, Arthur,
    always—though I'll own you never break, only chip a little. Have you
    met Clara Vane? Dr. Pembroke, my dear, physician to half the invalids of
    Cheltenham, and unmarried still, which I call criminal waste.

MISS VANE. (She smiles at Mrs. Ashworth, not at Dr. Pembroke) Criminal
    waste indeed, Mrs. Ashworth—though I wonder, is it beauty you prize in
    your collection, or the courage to be strange in company that punishes
    it?

        (A silence, which nobody moves to fill.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE TWO

The conservatory. Continuous. Lights up on MRS. MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Marchmont slips away from Mr. Strake's story to
stand among the ferns, and Dr. Pembroke follows her out, unable to help
himself.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

DR. PEMBROKE. (stands a little behind her, not touching) You left before
    the end of Strake's tale, Helena. The garden is dark and gives no
    answers—will you tell me instead what in it troubled you?

MRS. MARCHMONT. (does not turn from the glass) It was nothing, Arthur
    — only a turn of phrase from Mr. Strake, about letters that find their
    way back to those who sent them. One wonders whether such things are
    merely fancy, or something rather more attentive.

DR. PEMBROKE. (steps beside her, watching her reflection in the glass,
    not her face) Attentive, or merely idle chatter dressed as wit —
    Strake deals in both. But you do not colour like that over idle chatter,
    Helena. What letter was it recalled to you?

MRS. MARCHMONT. (does not turn to face him) There was no letter, Arthur
    — only the thought that what we destroy does not always consent to
    stay destroyed. Is that not worth pondering, apart from any séance or
    sitting?

        [Spotlight: DR. PEMBROKE]

DR. PEMBROKE. (watches her reflection, not the garden) Then it is
    Strake's phrase that unsettles you, not its truth — though I confess I
    wonder what he meant by it. Not that his meaning is any concern of mine,
    Helena; only yours.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (turns from the glass at last, meeting his eyes) You are
    kind to leave it mine, Arthur — kinder than you know. I have grown
    weary of being asked what troubles me, when the asking is itself the
    trouble.

        (Enter MR. STRAKE.)

MR. STRAKE. (glances between them, not yet looking at the garden)
    Arthur, you've abandoned Clara mid-argument to play physician
    here—Helena's only quiet, not fainting. What is it you think you're
    guarding against?

        [Spotlight: MR. STRAKE]

DR. PEMBROKE. (turns fully from the glass to face Strake) Guarding
    against nothing, Julian — merely curious what you meant by your
    phrase, about things destroyed not staying so. It was odd enough to
    unsettle Helena; I should like to hear it plainly.

MR. STRAKE. (does not look at Helena) Arthur, that's rather good—the
    doctor's concern, the friend's alarm, all in one breath, and never once
    betraying which you mean. I call that a fine performance, truly—I
    don't say it unkindly.

        (ARTHUR falls silent, color rising in his face; HELENA studies
        him as though reading a new sign; JULIAN lets the silence
        stretch, smiling faintly.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE THREE

The library. Continuous. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MISS VANE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth corners Miss Vane in the library for a
comfortable gossip, quite unaware of what she is about to give away.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

MRS. ASHWORTH. (draws Miss Vane toward the escritoire by the window)
    Strange in company that punishes it—oh, you clever creature, you have
    found me out entirely. Come, leave them to their teasing, and let me
    show you my little treasures here—far better company than a room full
    of chattering fools.

MISS VANE. (glances at the escritoire, then away, toward the cabinet
    beyond) Treasures, Mrs. Ashworth? Now you tempt me sorely. Is it true
    you have a fan that once belonged to a duchess—I heard it whispered,
    and thought it too delicious not to ask.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (crosses to the escritoire and lays a hand upon it) The
    fan, yes—poor thing, moth-eaten now, but a duchess did carry it, or so
    I was told and choose to believe. Come, it lives just here, in the
    escritoire, along with all my dearest secrets.

MISS VANE. (She steps half between Mrs. Ashworth and the escritoire,
    admiring, not looking down) Secrets and a duchess's fan
    together—however do you resist opening it every hour? Do tell me of
    the duchess, Mrs. Ashworth—was she wicked, or only unhappy? I always
    find the two go together.

        (Enter MRS. MARCHMONT.)

MRS. MARCHMONT. (enters, glancing briefly toward the little escritoire
    in the corner) Lavinia, you look like a woman with secrets to spill —
    what treasures have you been promising Clara while I lingered in the
    conservatory?

MRS. ASHWORTH. (pats the escritoire fondly, not noticing Miss Vane's
    stillness) Only my little hoard, Helena — a duchess's fan, some
    pressed flowers, and your dear letters, still tucked safe in there where
    you lent them to me — I keep forgetting to send them back!

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (glances at Miss Vane, who does not meet her eye) How
    careless of me to have lent them at all, Lavinia — letters want
    tending, not hoarding. Might I carry them home tonight? I should hate to
    think of them growing dusty among the fans.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (fusses at the escritoire's little lock, not looking up)
    Of course you shall, dearest — how good of you to remind me, I am
    dreadfully forgetful. The key sticks so; give me but a moment and I'll
    have them out for you directly.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (watches the lock, not Lavinia's hands) The key sticks
    so — how strange, Lavinia. Almost as though something meant to keep
    them from me a little longer.

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

MISS VANE. (She presses a hand to her own arm, as if chilled, stepping
    back from the escritoire) Forgive me—I felt the room turn suddenly
    cold, just there by the desk, as though something passed close between
    us. Do you feel it, Mrs. Marchmont?

        (MRS. ASHWORTH's fingers freeze on the stuck lock; MRS.
        MARCHMONT holds MISS VANE's eyes, waiting; the letters stay
        sealed inside the escritoire.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE FOUR

The drawing-room. Later. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MRS. MARCHMONT, DR.
PEMBROKE, MISS VANE, MR. STRAKE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth, Mrs. Marchmont, Dr. Pembroke, Miss Vane
and Mr. Strake gather in the drawing-room, where Lavinia's talk turns,
as it always does, to the one remarkable person in the house she has not
yet shown anyone.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

        (Enter CRUTTWELL, large and florid, a short strap in one hand;
        with the other he holds the sleeve of AMBROSE: seventy, a black
        coat too large for him, his hat held in both hands.)

MRS. ASHWORTH. (turns from the escritoire toward the door, visibly
    relieved) Oh—but here is my dear Ambrose at last! Mr. Strake, you must
    know him properly: my husband's brother, a clergyman, lately of Bengal,
    now living quietly here with us.

CRUTTWELL. (Takes Ambrose's hat firmly, keeping two fingers hooked in
    his sleeve) Ladies, gentlemen—Barnabas Cruttwell, formerly of the
    county asylum at Gloucester, now sole keeper to this good gentleman, day
    and night, these three years past, at no small cost to my own rest.
    Come, Ambrose, your hat. There. Now—think, Ambrose. Think for the
    company.

AMBROSE. (He blinks toward the window, hands fluttering as if still
    holding the hat) The mind, you must understand, the mind is a punkah, or
    was it a wicket, the twenty-second Article concerning Purgatory being a
    fond thing vainly invented, vainly, and the 4:15 from Cheltenham to
    Gloucester does not stop at Churchdown on Tuesdays, or does it, I
    forget, the liver being the seat of melancholy as the ancients held
    though Dr. Pembroke will correct me, the ancients held many things, the
    sun over Barrackpore at noon strikes down through the pith helmet and
    into the very seat of reason, I remember Colonel Fanshawe's innings at
    the Maidan, forty-one runs and not out, not out, as we are none of us
    out until the last trump, the Athanasian Creed being read only on
    certain days appointed, appointed, the Theosophists say the soul travels
    in circles like the 9:10 up line, round and round the Wheel, Madame
    Blavatsky I never met though the Bishop spoke of her with some heat,
    some heat, and the barometer is falling, is falling, I said to the
    punkah-wallah mind the glass, mind the glass, for Justification by Faith
    Only is a most wholesome doctrine and very full of comfort as the
    Article saith, comfort, the wicket was slow that year at Barrackpore,
    slow and low, the ball would not rise, would not rise, and the fever
    came with the rains as it always came, always came, and I wrote it down
    in the register, the deaths and the births and the overs bowled, overs
    bowled, the mind is a punkah, mind the glass, not out, not out, comfort,
    comfort, not out.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (does not look at Ambrose again, turns briskly toward the
    sideboard) There now, Ambrose, you have quite dazzled us — but you'll
    tire yourself. Mr. Cruttwell, do sit him by the fire and see he has his
    tea. Ices, anyone? Clara, you look quite pale.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (steps toward Ambrose, ignoring the sideboard entirely)
    Wait — do not take him away yet. 'Not out, not out' — he said it
    twice, as though it mattered more than the rest. What did he mean by it,
    Mr. Cruttwell?

DR. PEMBROKE. (steps between Helena and Ambrose, voice sharper than he
    intends) Helena — that is not a message, it is aphasia. A mind
    unravelling snatches at whatever lies nearest: cricket, catechism,
    Theosophy, all one to it. To read prophecy there is not reverence, it is
    cruelty dressed as wonder.

MISS VANE. (She does not look at Pembroke; she looks toward Ambrose,
    still) Cruelty or not, Doctor, I have never heard anything so beautiful
    spoken in a drawing-room — not out, not out — I do not think a mind
    wholly gone could reach for such music.

MR. STRAKE. (does not look at anyone in particular, his usual ease gone)
    No—not music, Clara, and not prophecy either. Only a man once
    brilliant, counting runs he can no longer finish. I find I haven't the
    wit to make anything clever of it.

CRUTTWELL. (Takes up Ambrose's hat, tightens two fingers in his sleeve,
    steers him toward the door) There, there—quite enough wonder for one
    evening; the doctor's word for it is aphasia, mine is a night's sleep
    gone begging, and between the two of us poor Ambrose gets no rest at
    all. Come, sir, your hat. We'll have that tea by the fire, away from
    clever tongues. Good night to the company—think kindly of the man who
    bears him.

        (Exeunt CRUTTWELL and AMBROSE, the one leading the other by the
        sleeve. A silence.)

MRS. ASHWORTH. (turns firmly from the door toward the sideboard, ices in
    hand) Good night, Mr. Cruttwell, and bless you for your patience. Come,
    Clara, you are quite white — a little cup will do you good, and you
    too, Doctor, you have earned it.

        (CRUTTWELL steers AMBROSE through the door; HELENA stares after
        it, unanswered, as MRS. ASHWORTH presses an ice into CLARA's
        hand and talks brightly on.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE FIVE

The library. Later. Lights up on MRS. MARCHMONT, MRS. ASHWORTH.

[Director's note: Mrs. Marchmont and Mrs. Ashworth meet in the library,
where Lavinia has at last brought down the letters, and everything
follows from that.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

MRS. ASHWORTH. (fumbles again at the escritoire, not meeting Helena's
    eye) Poor Ambrose—paraded like a curiosity for that Strake man to
    marvel at, and then all of us properly scolded for it. It quite turns my
    stomach, Helena. Now—where was I—this wretched lock—

MRS. MARCHMONT. (takes Lavinia's hand from the escritoire, gently) Never
    mind the lock a moment, Lavinia. You are shaken — sit down. It was a
    hard thing to watch, and harder still for you, who loves him.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (does not let go of the packet of letters) You are too
    good to me, Helena — always were. It is only Ambrose, and the lock,
    and the silliness of the evening pressing on me all at once. Sit with me
    a moment, do.

        (Enter MISS VANE.)

MISS VANE. (does not look at the packet in Helena's hands) Did I? I
    scarce recall it now, Mrs. Ashworth — the day was all flowers and
    chairs and fetching. Was there truly so very much to sort through?

MRS. ASHWORTH. (does not release the packet, smiling too brightly at
    Helena) Oh, there was a great deal, Clara dear, and you so quick and
    neat about it too — I always say you'd have made a splendid little
    secretary, for all your people think you too delicate for work.

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (Loosens the ribbon slightly, does not open the packet)
    Blue again — I keep noticing it tonight, everywhere I turn. Ambrose's
    collar, the ices, and now this ribbon round my own letters. I wonder if
    a colour can follow one about the room like that, or if I have only
    begun to see what was always there.

        (Enter DR. PEMBROKE.)

DR. PEMBROKE. (watches her face, not the packet in her hands) Helena,
    you have gone quite still. Miss Vane helped sort your letters, you say
    — the very afternoon before the sitting? That seems worth remembering,
    whatever the evening pretended afterward to reveal.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (Sets the packet down, still not opening it) Arthur, you
    make it sound a conspiracy. Clara sorted papers as a kindness — there
    is no crime in tidiness. I will not have her doubted for helping an old
    woman who cannot keep her own drawers in order.

        (Enter MR. STRAKE.)

MR. STRAKE. (watches her hands on the packet, not the letters
    themselves) Helena—you've gone quite still, holding those. Not bad
    news in them, I hope? You looked easier a moment ago than you do now.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (Her fingers tighten briefly on the packet before she
    lets it rest on her knee) No bad news, Julian — only old news, and old
    news has a way of feeling heavier than new. It is the ribbon, I think,
    that made me pause — such a particular blue. One notices these small
    persistences, does one not?

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

        (A silence, which nobody moves to fill.)

        (Lights down.)
