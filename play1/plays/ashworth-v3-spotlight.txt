AN EVENING AT MRS. ASHWORTH'S

A play in five scenes. The Etheridge Circle, a fortnight on.

PERSONS OF THE PLAY
    MRS. ASHWORTH, Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth
    MRS. MARCHMONT, Mrs. Helena Marchmont
    DR. PEMBROKE, Dr. Arthur Pembroke
    MISS VANE, Miss Clara Vane
    MR. STRAKE, Mr. Julian Strake
    AMBROSE, The Reverend Ambrose Ashworth
    CRUTTWELL, Mr. Barnabas Cruttwell

Rooms of Mrs. Lavinia Ashworth's house in Lansdown, Cheltenham. An
evening in the last week of October, 1891, a fortnight after the sitting
at which Miss Vane, in trance, spoke of a blue ribbon. Each scene is
played in one room, furnished lightly: a sideboard with champagne cup,
claret cup, sherry, ices and sandwiches; a few chairs nobody uses for
long; doors right and left. The fireplace and the window are downstage,
in the fourth wall, so that a character who warms her hands or looks
into the garden looks out at the audience. Mrs. Ashworth has asked the
same people again, "so that we may all be comfortable together." They
have all known one another for years, and are fond of one another, which
is what makes the evening dangerous. Upstairs lives her late husband's
brother, the Reverend Ambrose Ashworth, formerly a chaplain in Bengal,
who does not come down, and with him the man she pays to manage him, Mr.
Barnabas Cruttwell, lately of the asylum at Gloucester.

SCENE ONE

The drawing-room. Continuous. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MR. STRAKE,
MRS. MARCHMONT.

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth receives Mr. Strake and Mrs. Marchmont
in the drawing-room, where Strake is halfway through an account of a
Blavatskyite séance he attended in London.]

        [Spotlight: MR. STRAKE]

MR. STRAKE. (raises his glass toward the sideboard, smiling) Lavinia,
    this champagne cup is a masterpiece of deception—one swears it's
    champagne, when I'd wager my editor's patience it's chiefly hock and
    borage. Now, shall I tell you of Mahatmas?

MRS. ASHWORTH. (beckons Helena closer with her fan) Oh, borage indeed,
    Mr. Strake, you flatterer—do go on! Mahatmas, you say? Helena, do come
    nearer, you shall want to hear every word of this, I am quite sure.

MR. STRAKE. (Watches Helena's face rather than Lavinia's as he speaks)
    Very well—picture the room dim, thick with sandalwood smoke, thirty of
    us breathless round a mahogany table that presently rose a clear foot
    into the air, quite unassisted, or so we were assured. The raps came
    sharp as a woodpecker settling accounts.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (watches him eagerly, fan stilled in her hand) A foot in
    the air, unassisted! Mr. Strake, and did you look beneath the table
    yourself, or were you too transported by the sandalwood to think of
    trickery at all?

MR. STRAKE. (turns from Lavinia to smile at Helena) I confess I did not
    stoop to look, Lavinia—one doesn't, in company, any more than one
    lifts a hostess's tablecloth to count the silver. Though I'll say this:
    Helena listens with such perfect, trembling belief, it is nearly as fine
    a performance as the table's.

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

        (Enter DR. PEMBROKE.)

DR. PEMBROKE. (watches her face, not Strake's) You are very quiet,
    Helena. Strake's Mahatmas seem to have found a more attentive listener
    in you than in me — what is it in the tale that holds you?

MRS. MARCHMONT. (does not quite meet his eyes) It is merely the phrase,
    Arthur—"settling accounts." A curious turn of speech for Mr. Strake to
    have chosen, is it not, quite unprompted? One notices such things,
    philosophically speaking.

DR. PEMBROKE. (steps nearer, voice lowered beneath the general chatter)
    Philosophically speaking, Helena, you have gone rather pale over a mere
    turn of speech. Settling accounts with whom, I wonder — the departed,
    or the living? You need not answer if you'd rather not.

        [Spotlight: DR. PEMBROKE]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (does not look at him; smooths her skirt) With neither,
    Arthur. You mistake a listener's stillness for something graver than it
    is—I am merely attentive, as anyone would be to so diverting a tale.
    Do go on, Mr. Strake; you had reached the raps.

        (MRS. ASHWORTH turns brightly to STRAKE: "Of course, you know
        Dr. Pembroke attended poor Helena's boy—right to the end."
        Silence. HELENA does not move.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE TWO

The conservatory. Moments later. Lights up on MISS VANE, MR. STRAKE,
MRS. ASHWORTH.

[Director's note: Mr. Strake follows Miss Vane into the conservatory,
admiring her craft with a connoisseur's warmth she cannot quite trust,
while Mrs. Ashworth beams over them both.]

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

MR. STRAKE. (Finds her among the ferns, keeps a polite distance) Miss
    Vane—you've fled rather faster than the chill in that room warranted.
    Was it the sandalwood, or did some part of my woodpecker's raps offend a
    professional ear? You've the look of a woman totting up a bill.

MISS VANE. (touches her own arm, as if chilled) A bill, Mr. Strake? How
    unromantic you make me sound. I only stepped away because—there, do
    you feel it?—a coldness came over the room quite suddenly, and I
    thought it kinder to withdraw than to shiver in company.

MR. STRAKE. A coldness—truly? I felt nothing, but then I was watching
    Helena's face rather than the air. Tell me plainly, Miss Vane: do you
    believe such a thing can come over a room, or do you only report that
    others believe it?

MISS VANE. I believe a room can hold a feeling the way a cellar holds
    damp, Mr. Strake—not magic, only sensitivity. Some of us are
    thinner-skinned to it, that is all. I do not choose what comes over me;
    I only choose not to hide it.

MR. STRAKE. Not hide it—that's the interesting part, Miss Vane. Most
    people hide a great deal, in my experience, and call it manners. What is
    it costs you, to let a room see you shiver?

MISS VANE. (does not look away from him) It costs me the safety of being
    ordinary, Mr. Strake. A plain woman may weep or freeze and no one
    remarks it; I must earn my keep by feeling things loudly enough for a
    room to notice.

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

        (Enter MRS. MARCHMONT.)

MRS. MARCHMONT. (glances warmly at Clara, then to Lavinia) Lavinia, you
    look quite triumphant—have you found some new scheme to execute upon
    us? Clara has only just found the air of the ferns; do not fluster her
    so soon.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (beams, quite oblivious to Clara's stillness) Oh but you
    two have not been properly introduced! Mr. Strake writes for the papers,
    my dear—exposes frauds and tables both, quite ruthlessly—and Miss
    Vane lodges alone in Suffolk Road, no family to speak of. Is that not
    delightful, how much you already have in common?

MISS VANE. (smiles, but her eyes stay on Lavinia a beat too long) How
    good of you to think of matches for me, Mrs. Ashworth—though I confess
    I hardly know what a lady with no family and a man who unmasks frauds
    should have in common, beyond good manners.

        [Spotlight: MR. STRAKE]

        (MISS VANE gasps at a sudden 'presence' and turns from STRAKE as
        MRS. ASHWORTH cries that Clara knew every room here before the
        sitting.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE THREE

The conservatory. Moments later. Lights up on MRS. MARCHMONT, MISS VANE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Marchmont finds herself alone with Miss Vane
among the ferns, Mrs. Ashworth having steered them together and then
been called away, and she means to ask, quietly, about the blue ribbon.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (leans slightly toward Clara, hands not quite still) "No
    family to speak of"—Lavinia's very words, and yours after her. A
    curious phrase to hear twice in one breath, Miss Vane. One begins to
    wonder whether such repetitions are entirely chance.

MISS VANE. (watches Helena's hands, not her face) Chance,
    surely—though I own the phrase fits me well enough. You look as though
    it is not my family troubles you, Mrs. Marchmont, but some question
    nearer home. What is it you wish to ask me?

MRS. MARCHMONT. (Her hands twist the fringe of her shawl) Only
    this—that in philosophy nothing is chance, Miss Vane. A word repeated,
    a phrase caught twice, these are not accidents but patterns, and
    patterns must mean something. I only wish to understand what.

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

MISS VANE. (shivers, drawing her shawl close, though the conservatory is
    warm) How strange—there, that chill again, just as you spoke of
    patterns. Forgive me, Mrs. Marchmont, my family is old ground, worn
    thin; I would rather not walk it twice in an evening.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (reaches out, takes Clara's cold hand between her own)
    Forgive me, my dear—I have been thoughtless, pressing where you asked
    me not to. Come, sit by me here instead, and tell me nothing of family
    at all. What do you love, Miss Vane? Tell me only that.

MISS VANE. (does not pull her hand away; looks down at it) What I love?
    ...Kindness, Mrs. Marchmont—plain kindness, freely given, which I have
    not always had cause to expect. You are gentler with me than I deserve,
    and I do not know how to answer it properly.

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (presses Clara's hand gently, then releases it) Then you
    shall have it freely from me, my dear, and ask nothing in return—not
    even that you explain yourself. I had almost forgotten what it was, to
    sit quietly with someone and want nothing proved.

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

        (Enter DR. PEMBROKE.)

DR. PEMBROKE. Miss Vane, tell me honestly — when you sense a presence,
    is it a feeling in the body, or a thought that arrives already formed? I
    ask as a physician, not a sceptic, for once.

        [Spotlight: DR. PEMBROKE]

MISS VANE. (watches him steadily, hands folded) A feeling first, Dr.
    Pembroke—like a draught felt before one sees the window open. The
    thought comes after, dressed in words. Why do you ask as a physician
    tonight, and not as a sceptic?

        (CLARA'S lips part to answer DR. PEMBROKE, but the door bangs
        open — JULIAN STRAKE leans in, grinning, asking who has seen
        Cruttwell's whistle.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE FOUR

The drawing-room. Moments later. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MRS.
MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE, MR. STRAKE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth, Mrs. Marchmont, Dr. Pembroke and Mr.
Strake gather in the drawing-room, where Mrs. Ashworth, flushed and
delighted with how the evening is going, proposes to bring her
brother-in-law down for once.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. ASHWORTH]

        (Enter CRUTTWELL, large and florid, a short strap in one hand;
        with the other he holds the sleeve of AMBROSE: seventy, a black
        coat too large for him, his hat held in both hands.)

MRS. ASHWORTH. (beams, drawing Ambrose forward by the sleeve) Ah, here
    he is—my husband, the Reverend Ashworth, forty years in the pulpit at
    Leamington before we retired here, Mr. Strake. Ambrose, this is the
    gentleman who has seen tables rise a foot in the air!

CRUTTWELL. (holds Ambrose's sleeve firm, removing his hat with a
    flourish) Ladies, gentlemen—Barnabas Cruttwell, sir, formerly of the
    county asylum at Gloucester, now sole keeper to this poor gentleman, day
    and night, night and day. Little sleep I get of it, Mr. Strake, little
    enough. (takes Ambrose's hat) Now then, Reverend—think, Ambrose. Think
    for the company.

AMBROSE. (He is led to the good chair and stands blinking, hatless) The
    wicket at Barrackpore was slow after the rains and the Article Nine on
    Original Sin is likewise slow to take root in a heathen soil though the
    bowling was straight and the sun came down like a brass dish upon the
    skull and the skull being porous as the Bishop of Madras once observed
    retains heat as a kettle retains its whistle and the 4:15 from
    Paddington does not stop at Cheltenham on Thursdays owing to engineering
    works upon the line which is a mercy for the line if not for the
    traveller and the humours of the body being four as the old physic
    taught though Dr. Pembroke will tell you otherwise with his stethoscope
    and his modern notions and I do not say he is wrong only that the bile
    was black in Bengal and black bile makes for melancholy and melancholy
    makes for theosophical speculation upon the transmigration of souls
    which is no part of the Articles though it creeps in at the window like
    a moth toward candle and the candle being Christ the moth is singed and
    falls and the fielding was slack that season, very slack, and the
    Collector said so, and the barometer stood at twenty-nine point six
    which portends rain or the absence of rain, one cannot be certain, the
    mercury being a coy informant, and the Nicene formula does not mention
    cricket nor does cricket mention the Nicene formula and yet both require
    patience, patience, patience under a hot sun, under a white sun, the sun
    that struck me, that struck me, that struck me down at Barrackpore, the
    wicket was slow, the wicket was slow, patience, patience, the 4:15, the
    4:15, patience.

MRS. ASHWORTH. (does not look at Ambrose; already turning toward the
    sideboard) Well! How he does run on—forty years of sermons will do
    that to a man. Barnabas, do sit him by the fire and see he takes some
    cup; I think we have all had cricket enough for one evening.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (presses a hand to her breastbone, still listening) No,
    Lavinia—that was not running on, that was poetry. "Melancholy makes
    for theosophical speculation upon the transmigration of souls"—he
    might have reached into my own thoughts and drawn them out entire.

DR. PEMBROKE. (crosses to Ambrose, takes his wrist as if to feel the
    pulse) That is not poetry, Helena — that is a sun-struck brain running
    loose over broken country, seizing on theosophy as it seized on cricket
    and railway timetables. Do not dress a sick man's disorder as
    revelation; it does him and you a disservice.

MR. STRAKE. (leans toward Ambrose, forgetting Pembroke's hand on the
    pulse) No, wait—say that again, Reverend, the part about the moth and
    the candle being Christ. That wasn't a wandering wit, that was a man
    arriving somewhere. Pembroke, you'd silence him before he finishes the
    sentence—I want the rest of it.

CRUTTWELL. (pulls Ambrose firmly from the chair toward the door) That
    will do, sir—that will do. (claps the hat upon Ambrose's head,
    gripping the sleeve) Come, Reverend, your keeper knows when the well
    runs dry. Ladies, gentlemen, forgive us—it is I who pay for these
    flights, in broken nights and a temper not my own; but there, one does
    not abandon a soul for want of thanks.

        (Exit CRUTTWELL, toward the hall.)

        (Exeunt CRUTTWELL and AMBROSE, the one leading the other by the
        sleeve. A silence.)

        (Enter MISS VANE.)

MISS VANE. (glances toward the stairs, uneasy) Bring him down, Mrs.
    Ashworth? Truly? Forgive me—I had understood the Reverend was unwell.
    Does he take pleasure in company, even when he is not quite himself?

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (turns from Ambrose's chair to look at Clara) No, my
    dear, I think he does not truly know we are here at all—it is his own
    mind he keeps company with, not ours. It seems a hard mercy to bring him
    down for sport.

        [Spotlight: DR. PEMBROKE]

        (CRUTTWELL steers AMBROSE through the doorway; it clicks shut.
        No one speaks. MRS. ASHWORTH lifts the champagne cup, then sets
        it down, untouched.)

        (Lights down.)

SCENE FIVE

The drawing-room. Moments later. Lights up on MRS. ASHWORTH, MRS.
MARCHMONT, DR. PEMBROKE, MISS VANE, MR. STRAKE.

[Director's note: Mrs. Ashworth, Mrs. Marchmont, Dr. Pembroke, Miss Vane
and Mr. Strake gather in the drawing-room, still shaken by what was
heard upstairs, and Helena at last asks Clara, plainly, how she knew
about the ribbon.]

        [Spotlight: MRS. MARCHMONT]

MRS. MARCHMONT. (turns from the cup to face Clara directly, waiting)
    Clara—look there, the cup stands full, no one has touched it all
    evening, as though we are none of us permitted to drink until something
    is finished. Tell me truly: did those words come to you of themselves,
    or did you feel me wishing them into your mouth?

MISS VANE. (does not look away from Helena, her hands still) I hardly
    know how to answer you truly, Mrs. Marchmont. I do not think you wished
    it into my mouth—but tell me, would you rather it had come from you,
    or from somewhere further off than either of us?

MRS. MARCHMONT. (does not look at the cup now, only at Clara) From
    further off, I think—though I could not tell you why I hope it, only
    that I could not bear it to be merely my own longing dressed up and sent
    back to me. That would be the cruellest trick of all.

        [Spotlight: MISS VANE]

MISS VANE. (takes Helena's hand again, without being asked) Then let it
    comfort you as truth, however it came, Mrs. Marchmont—for I think a
    thing may be true in its kindness even where its road to us is strange.
    I would not have you doubt the comfort for doubting me.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (keeps hold of Clara's hand, searching her face) You are
    gentler with me than I deserve, Clara. Tell me—when it comes to you,
    this... further off place—does it ever frighten you? Or have you long
    since made your peace with hearing what others cannot?

MISS VANE. (sways, hand to her brow, reaching blindly for the back of a
    chair) It does frighten me—there, even now, a coldness pressing close
    behind me, as if someone stood too near… forgive me, Mrs. Marchmont,
    the room turns strange, I must sit.

MRS. MARCHMONT. (catches Clara's arm, guiding her firmly into the chair)
    Clara—here, sit, you've gone quite white. Never mind further off
    places now; only breathe. Arthur, come, she is faint—

        [Spotlight: MR. STRAKE]

MR. STRAKE. (leans forward, notebook mind plainly at work despite
    himself) Mind the pulse, Pembroke, but mind the timing too—fear
    arriving precisely at the question's edge, not a beat before nor after.
    That is not hysteria, that is craft, and rather fine craft at that.

DR. PEMBROKE. (takes Clara's wrist properly now, two fingers, watching
    her face) Craft or not, her pulse is quick and thready. Clara, look at
    me — count backward from twenty, slowly. Julian, hold your theories a
    moment; the girl needs air, not an audience.

MR. STRAKE. (steps back, notebook mind briefly, genuinely, forgotten)
    Yes—forgive me, Arthur, you're quite right. Clara, don't mind me at
    all, I talk nonsense when I'm interested. Is there water? Someone fetch
    water.

        [Spotlight: DR. PEMBROKE]

        (LAVINIA, pressing water into Clara's hand, says brightly: "Of
        course she knew of the ribbon, dear — I showed her your
        letters myself, the ones I never returned!" No one moves. No one
        answers.)

        (Lights down.)


                              CURTAIN.

  goal: Establish Strake's admiration for the craft of the London séance as connoisseurship rather than exposure, with Helena unable to resist hearing it as a possible sign, before Dr. Pembroke's arrival and Lavinia's introduction turns the temperature cold.
